CHAPTER 01 · 2024 · TEACHING-TEAM MEMBER

Coding Summer CampBefore I taught code, I had to make it feel real.

I arrived in Kon Tum thinking the technical content was the hard part. The classroom taught me otherwise. A block of Scratch code could be correct and still fail as an explanation. My real job became reducing the distance between an unfamiliar idea and the confidence to try it.

MAKE IT VISIBLE→LET THEM TRY→DEBUG TOGETHER
Enter the classroom ↓

We built the lesson before we taught it.

Our team prepared physical learning aids, color-coded pieces, Scratch demonstrations, and rehearsed explanations before students entered the room. The goal was simple: make an abstract instruction visible enough that a learner could connect it to something concrete.

I started seeing teaching as another design problem: if the learner is confused, the interface needs to change.

The classroom became a prototype, too.

Once students started working, the lesson stopped being a script. I moved between the board, individual screens, and small groups, watching where people hesitated and changing the explanation in real time.

Robotics taught me to debug machines. Kon Tum taught me to debug explanations.
Beat 01 · Read the room

Start with the learner, not the slide.

I learned to watch faces, screens, and pauses for the moment an explanation stopped landing. The best next step was often smaller than what I had planned.

Beat 02 · Debug together

Do not take the mouse too quickly.

The fastest way to fix a project was to do it myself. The better way was to ask what the student expected, trace the logic together, and let them make the final change.

Beat 03 · Hand over ownership

The best moment was when I was no longer needed.

Students began comparing screens, presenting their work, pointing out blocks to one another, and turning our demonstration into their own experiments. That shift became my definition of a successful lesson.

The code was only half the story.

Confidence grew between activities too: in the way we invited quieter students in, joked outside the formal lesson, and coordinated as a teaching team. The more I taught, the less teaching felt like delivering information and the more it felt like building conditions around people.

What 2024 changed in me.

I entered the camp thinking technical knowledge was the thing I could give. I left understanding that access depends just as much on explanation, trust, and design.

Engineering lesson

A solution is only useful if someone can understand it.

I became more attentive to the interface between a system and the person meeting it for the first time.

Leadership lesson

Do not solve every problem for the learner.

Build enough structure that someone else can make the next decision and recover from a mistake themselves.

Service lesson

Lower the first barrier, then let curiosity do the rest.

A simpler demo or one patient debugging conversation can change “I cannot do this” into “let me try again.”

2024 in one sentence: I arrived wanting to explain technology clearly. I left wanting to create the conditions where another person felt confident enough to explore it without me.
