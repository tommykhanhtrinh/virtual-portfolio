A better starting point

This season began with more clarity.

High Stakes taught me how much a robot depends on the decisions made before the first part is assembled. In Push Back, I came in with a stronger sense of structure: a clearer drivetrain plan, more purposeful mechanism choices, and a deeper habit of learning through iteration rather than improvisation alone.

01

Start with the frame.

I paid more attention to the robot’s foundation: drive layout, weight balance, structure, and enough room for later mechanisms.

02

Build with the game in mind.

Instead of just making mechanisms move, every subsystem had to serve a match purpose: scoring, positioning, and repeatability under pressure.

03

Scrimmage as a real test.

SSIS Scrimmage was not just a warm-up. It was the first proof that our build, preparation, and team coordination could work in a real competition setting.

04

One chapter kept expanding.

What first looked like a five-event Push Back season eventually stretched all the way to Worlds. Each event changed the next version of the robot — and by the end, the learning expanded beyond mechanisms into software, strategy, driving, and an international robotics community.

Chapter 03 · Taiwan rebuild

We did not just tweak the robot — we changed the concept.

Before Taiwan Signature Event, we rebuilt again, but this time the change was deeper. We moved away from the earlier basket-bot direction and committed to a new S-bot concept that was lower, tighter, and more optimized for control, contact, and repeatability. That decision reflected one of the most important lessons in my growth: if a concept is no longer the best answer, you need the discipline to let it go and build a better one.

Reflection · what stayed with me

Taiwan changed how I think about iteration, versioning, and serious VEX engineering.

This chapter was valuable not only because it was international, but because it made earlier lessons concrete. I could finally see, in real time, why strong teams rebuild between events, why CAD and testing make later decisions easier, and why a robot should be treated as a sequence of evolving versions rather than one fixed machine.

Lesson 01

Iteration needs evidence.

It is not enough to say a robot feels better. Comparing versions through CAD, testing, and real field performance gives the rebuild direction and keeps improvement grounded in evidence.

Lesson 02

Top teams think in versions.

Watching teams in a harder international environment reinforced something I had started to notice before: high-level VEX teams do not cling to one build. They are ready to redesign, repackage, and rebuild from event to event.

Lesson 03

Engineering is also communication.

A more complex event also demanded clearer communication inside the team. As designs grow more advanced, everyone needs to understand the robot’s logic, not only their own isolated task.

Statement-of-purpose connection

Taiwan Signature Event perfectly matched one of the biggest ideas in my statement of purpose: I moved from a more freestyle building mindset to a more deliberate engineering design process — plan, CAD, test, revise, compare versions, and rebuild with purpose.

Pre-Worlds sprint · 6–8 February 2026

The shortest rebuild of the season was almost invisible.

There was very little time between the National Championship and the Vietnam Signature Event. A full mechanical rebuild would have cost us testing time, so we made a deliberate choice: freeze most of the hardware and move our attention to the parts of performance that could still change fastest — code, strategy, and driving.

What we rebuilt instead

We kept the metal.
We rebuilt the behavior.

The robot entering Vietnam Signature looked close to the National version, but our preparation was different. We spent the remaining time turning repeated runs into information: tune, observe, review, adjust. The goal was not a new-looking robot. The goal was a robot and drive team that made better decisions every match.
