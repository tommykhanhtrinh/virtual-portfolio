Coding Summer Camp 2024 · Kon Tum

Before I taught code, I had to make it feel real.

My first instinct was to prepare more content. The better answer was the opposite: make the idea simpler, more visual, and easier to touch. That changed the way I think about teaching — and later, the way I think about engineering.

01 / Before class

We built the lesson before we taught it.

Instead of beginning with a screen full of blocks, our team prepared physical and visual learning aids, color-coded pieces, Scratch demos, and practice explanations. The goal was to reduce the distance between an abstract instruction and something a student could immediately recognize.

I started seeing teaching as another design problem: if the learner is confused, the “interface” needs to change.

02 / In the room

The classroom became a prototype, too.

Once students started building, the lesson stopped being a script. I moved between the board, the screen, and individual laptops — watching where they hesitated, changing examples, and helping them trace problems one step at a time.

Robotics taught me to debug machines. Kon Tum taught me to debug explanations.
03 / The people around the code

A coding lesson became something bigger than code.

Once the sessions were running, I stopped thinking of teaching as standing at the front and delivering a perfect explanation. The real work happened in the transitions: noticing confusion, kneeling beside a laptop, changing the example, pulling the group back together, and keeping the room curious enough to try again.

The more I taught, the less the lesson felt like a presentation — and the more it felt like a system built around people.
Beat 01 · Read the room

Start with the learners, not the slides.

I could prepare instructions beforehand, but the classroom decided the pace. I learned to watch faces, notebooks, and screens for the moment an explanation stopped landing — then reset before confusion turned into silence.

Beat 02 · Debug together

Do not take the mouse too quickly.

The fastest way to “fix” a project was often to do it myself. The better way was slower: ask what the student expected, trace the logic together, and let them make the final change. That small handoff turned troubleshooting into ownership.

Beat 03 · Learning is social

Confidence grew between activities, too.

Not every useful moment happened inside Scratch. Shared classroom activities, quick explanations with our hands, and informal moments outside helped lower the distance between “teacher” and “student.” Once the room felt safer, asking questions became easier.

Beat 04 · The team is part of the system

Teaching well meant coordinating well.

We compared what worked, shared observations, and adjusted together. That made the camp feel less like a collection of individual presenters and more like one team learning how to serve the same students from different angles.

Beat 05 · Bring the energy back home

Service changed how I worked with my own team.

By the time we were back in the lab, I was paying more attention to group energy, clarity, and participation — the same variables I had learned to read in Kon Tum. Communication was no longer separate from technical work; it was part of whether the work could move at all.

What changed in me: I arrived wanting to explain technology clearly. I left more interested in something harder — creating the conditions where another person feels confident enough to explore it without me.
04 / Ownership

The best moment was when I was no longer needed.

Students began presenting, comparing screens, pointing out blocks to one another, and turning our demonstrations into their own experiments. By then, the room no longer felt like “us teaching them.” It felt like a shared workshop. That shift — from following instructions to owning the next step — became my definition of a successful lesson.

05 / What stayed with me

Service changed the way I build.

I entered the camp thinking technical knowledge was the thing I could give. I left understanding that access depends just as much on explanation, trust, and design.

Engineering lesson

A solution is only useful if someone can understand it.

Whether I am designing a robot mechanism or a learning activity, I now pay more attention to the interface between the system and the person using it.

Leadership lesson

Do not solve every problem for the team.

The stronger move is to build enough structure that other people can make decisions, test ideas, and recover from mistakes themselves.

Service lesson

Lower the first barrier, then let curiosity do the rest.

A visual prop, a simpler demo, or one patient debugging conversation can be the difference between “I cannot code” and “let me try one more thing.”

More service chapters

This was only the beginning.

The same idea kept returning in later projects: make technical knowledge easier to enter, then give people enough ownership to take it further than I could alone.
